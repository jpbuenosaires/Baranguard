        1 Introduction

        1.1 Background of the Study

        The rapid advancement of information technology has transformed the way organizations manage data and coordinate emergency responses, shifting from localized, paper-based protocols to highly integrated, intelligence-driven digital frameworks. Globally, the demand for robust emergency response solutions is surging, driven by rising natural disasters and the need for interconnected public safety networks. Consequently, the global incident and emergency management market is projected to grow substantially through 2030 (MarketsandMarkets, 2024). This global technological shift aligns with the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3, which promotes good health and well-being; SDG 11, which advocates for sustainable, safe, and resilient cities; and SDG 16, which emphasizes the promotion of peace, justice, and effective, accountable institutions (United Nations, 2015).

        In the Philippines, a significant digital divide hampers the modernization of grassroots emergency management. While the Department of Information and Communications Technology (DICT) has spearheaded the e-Government Masterplan (eGMP) 2022–2025 to connect local government units (DICT, 2024), recent data from the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) indicate that only approximately 48.8 percent of households had internet access at home in 2024 (PSA, 2025), with far lower rates in some regions. At the grassroots level, the barangay serves as the primary planning and implementing unit of government policies, acting as the immediate responder to localized crises. Despite this mandate, a vast majority of Philippine barangays continue to rely on manual, handwritten logbooks for their blotter and emergency dispatch systems. These manual processes are inefficient, susceptible to physical data loss, and pose data privacy risks under Republic Act No. 10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012) due to the unprotected handling of sensitive citizen information (National Privacy Commission, 2012).

        This operational bottleneck is evident in the Municipality of Pilar, a first-class coastal municipality in the province of Sorsogon with a population of 76,908 (PhilAtlas, 2024). The municipality is geographically vulnerable to severe weather disturbances, including the destructive impacts of recent typhoons and fires, which disrupt local communication lines. Within Pilar, the communities of Barangay Dao (population 2,485), Barangay Marifosque (population 2,473), Barangay Binanuahan (population 1,889), and Barangay Banuyo (population 3,809) exemplify the challenges of rural public safety administration. In these barangays, field responders (tanods) lack real-time geographical tracking and face communication breakdowns when terrestrial IP networks fail during calamities.

        While several web-based barangay information systems currently exist in the local market, they predominantly function as static administrative repositories (Tosper et al., 2025). These existing solutions present operational limitations in rural contexts because they lack offline-first operational resilience and rely on continuous internet connectivity to maintain core dispatch workflows. This study proposes Baranguard: Barangay Intelligence and Emergency Dispatch System, an offline-first, local-server-based platform that joins three components: a GIS-enabled web command center for the Chief Tanod and barangay officials, an offline-first Android application for tanods, and an SMS best-effort fallback that carries emergency alerts when broadband fails.

        Baranguard is positioned as a complement to, not a replacement for, the Barangay Information Management System (BIMS) of the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG), whose Katarungang Pambarangay module supports the case process (DILG, 2025). Barangays continue to keep the blotter and Lupon records in their own binders, and Baranguard holds no case registry. It supports compliance with RA 10173 through role-based access, per-barangay data isolation, encryption of SMS payloads, an audit log that records identifiers only, and a retention rule that permanently destroys confidential incident narratives after one year.

        1.2 Objectives of the Study

        This study aimed to design and develop Baranguard, a local-server-based, offline-first barangay intelligence and emergency dispatch system tailored for Barangays Dao, Binanuahan, Marifosque, and Banuyo in Pilar, Sorsogon. Specifically, the study aimed:

        1. To design a GIS-enabled web-based command center that centralizes incident records, tracks responder locations in near-real-time, and generates historical incident heatmaps.
        2. To develop an offline-first mobile operations application for barangay tanods capable of capturing incident details without internet access and synchronizing queued records to the barangay command center server upon reconnection.
        3. To implement an SMS best-effort fallback communication protocol that automatically transmits emergency (SOS) alerts with the last known coordinates from field responders when broadband networks fail under partial-outage conditions.
        4. To evaluate the system's technical quality, usability, and performance using the ISO/IEC 25010:2023 software quality model.

        1.3 Significance of the Study

        Primary Beneficiaries. The direct users of the system, barangay tanods and the Chief Tanod who directs them, will experience a modernized, efficient workflow. The offline-first mobile application improves their daily tasks by allowing them to log incidents locally via SQLite caching, consult pre-cached offline map tiles for geographic orientation, receive dispatch assignments and, at night, dispatch offers, and raise an emergency alert that falls back to SMS during network outages. This is designed to reduce duplicated manual documentation effort and to shorten dispatch coordination time, the interval between an incident being received at the command center and a responder confirming deployment. Emergency response time in the full sense, measured to a responder's physical arrival, is determined largely by road conditions and responder availability and is not claimed as an outcome of this study.

        Administrators and Management. Local chief executives and barangay officials will benefit from enhanced, data-driven governance. The web-based command center provides historical heatmaps and statistical reports, enabling proactive decisions regarding resource deployment and patrol scheduling. Rosters, monthly accomplishment reports and school-zone reports are produced as A4 printouts that officials sign on paper, which keeps the system consistent with how barangay records are already authorized. The Barangay Secretary remains the custodian of confidential incident narratives, consistent with the Secretary's role as keeper of barangay records under Section 394(d) of Republic Act No. 7160 (the section names the records of the sangguniang barangay and the barangay assembly, so extending that custody to confidential incident narratives is a design decision of this study), and a named officer, not the software, remains accountable for every record. This arrangement supports compliance with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173).

        Future Researchers. The architectural framework of Baranguard serves as a reference for future IT capstone students and academic researchers. By demonstrating the practical deployment of an Agile Scrum framework, an offline-first mobile architecture with encrypted SQLite synchronization and device-signed writes, and a single-workstation local-server deployment that remains reachable through a persistent tunnel, this study contributes to the body of knowledge on resilient local e-governance systems.

        Community and Society. The residents of Barangays Dao, Binanuahan, Marifosque, and Banuyo will benefit from a safer, more responsive community environment. The system supports SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) by supporting faster coordination of emergency medical dispatch in underserved coastal communities; any effect on injury or mortality is not measured by this study. It further supports SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities) and SDG 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions), fostering greater social inclusion, transparent public service delivery, and elevated trust in local peacekeeping mechanisms (United Nations, 2015).

        1.4 Scope and Delimitations

        This study encompasses the development and deployment of a dual-platform incident management system, featuring an Android-based mobile application for field responders and a local network-based web application deployed on an administrative workstation. The system's core functionalities are a web-based command center equipped with GIS heatmaps, live responder tracking and a dispatch center; an offline-first tanod application; and an SMS best-effort fallback for emergency alerts. To ensure reliability in the field, the mobile application is designed with offline data caching and a three-state location behavior: where the device has an active network path, GPS coordinates are transmitted to the command center in near-real time (by polling); where the device is offline, coordinates are captured locally and queued for synchronization on reconnection; and where broadband has failed but cellular signal persists, an emergency alert with the last known coordinates may be transmitted over the SMS best-effort fallback channel. The fallback carries emergency (SOS) alerts only, not general incident reporting.

        The system implements four roles. The Admin account is the seat of the Chief Tanod, who dispatches tanods, manages users, devices, rosters and map packages, and reads the confidential incident narrative on the web. The Barangay Secretary is the Records and Reports Officer, who holds exclusive authority over the incident lifecycle (duplicate, invalid, cancelled, reopened) and the complainant's name, in keeping with the Secretary's role as the barangay's records officer (RA 7160, Sec. 394(d); a design decision of this study for incident records). The Punong Barangay holds oversight access to the dashboard, analytics, approvals and reports, and has no list of cases. Tanods operate on the Android application only and have no dashboard access. Approval authority, for example over a roster or an Annex D report, is a setting that the Chief Tanod grants to a named official, not a separate role. Residents do not use the system directly: walk-in, phone-call, Facebook and SMS reports are logged by staff as incidents, and each incident records the channel through which it arrived. Deployment and testing are bounded to Barangays Dao, Binanuahan, Marifosque, and Banuyo in the Municipality of Pilar, Sorsogon. The system's technical efficacy and user acceptance are evaluated using the ISO/IEC 25010:2023 software quality assessment model.

        To maintain developmental feasibility and to respect the legal position of the barangay's existing records, specific boundaries have been established. Baranguard is not a case registry and does not replace the DILG BIMS: it holds no electronic blotter and no Katarungang Pambarangay or Lupon records, and the barangays keep these in their own binders. The system excludes a payroll module for barangay staff, predictive case resolution algorithms, financial e-payment gateways for barangay clearances, resident profiling and certificate issuance. It uses no artificial intelligence of any kind, and complex computer vision, real-time video surveillance analysis and facial recognition are omitted. Direct mobile application access is restricted to authorized responders and tanods to prevent network spamming, thereby excluding general residents from the mobile platform. Envelope encryption of SMS payloads, per-device secret provisioning, replay-window validation, device-signed offline writes and an allow-listed audit log are implemented to support compliance with RA 10173; advanced biometric authentication and multi-factor authentication fall outside the developmental scope. The central database and web dashboard are hosted locally on an administrative workstation within the barangay command center, maintaining local area network (LAN) accessibility during internet blackouts while buffering field records locally on responder mobile devices. One cloud service is used: road routes are requested from OpenRouteService, which receives the origin and destination coordinates of a route and nothing else; map display and all other functions continue without it.

        1.4.1 Resilience Layer Limitations and Mitigations

        A critical architectural consideration is the behavior of the system under full communication blackout conditions, such as those experienced during a direct Category 4+ typhoon landfall. Historical precedent from Super Typhoon Rolly (Goni) in November 2020, which made its first landfall in Catanduanes at maximum sustained winds of 225 km/h and brought documented catastrophic disruption to the Bicol–Sorsogon region, including the province of Sorsogon, demonstrates that both broadband internet infrastructure and cellular tower networks can fail simultaneously. The NDRRMC's final situational report documented that 125 cities and municipalities were left without electricity after landfall, and telecommunications lines remained down across multiple Bicol provinces for days, with Globe and Smart signals described as "intermittent" in Albay and 13 municipalities in Camarines Sur reporting a complete loss of network coverage from both telecoms providers (NDRRMC, 2020). Under such conditions, the SMS fallback protocol, while valuable, operates as a best-effort bridge rather than a guaranteed channel. The SMS mechanism is empirically most effective in the more common partial-outage scenario, where cellular signal persists after broadband loss, an expectation rather than a guarantee, because whether text messages survive depends on whether power, backhaul or towers failed, and SMS itself can fail in severe disasters (Omar & Van Belle, 2023). The reliability of the fallback is therefore tested against the criterion in Section 3.3.4 and not assumed. Proposing a further cellular-based tertiary fallback would be architecturally redundant, as it would suffer the same failure mode.

        For full blackout scenarios where both broadband and cellular fail, the system's primary resilience mechanism is the offline-first mobile architecture. All incident records captured in the field by Tanods are preserved in the local SQLite database on the device to prevent loss caused by network disconnection. These records synchronize automatically via batch API payload to the local server database upon restoration of connectivity, whether through cellular data, broadband, or local Wi-Fi. Consequently, incident records are preserved locally during network outages and reconciled with the command center once communication is re-established. The Chief Tanod, operating the web dashboard during a full blackout, follows manual operational protocols until connectivity is restored, at which point Baranguard reconciles the offline queue. A further stated limit is that the whole system depends on one workstation: if it is powered off, only the device-local SMS alert to a backup contact still works, and protecting the workstation with a UPS and a backup internet line is a deployment decision outside this study. Future research may investigate the integration of store-and-forward radio mesh protocols as a non-cellular tertiary communication channel.

        Taken together, these architectural decisions reflect a deliberate design philosophy prioritizing local data preservation over real-time transmission guarantees. Rather than attempting to engineer around the physical constraints of typhoon-grade infrastructure disruption, a challenge that affects primary telecommunications networks, Baranguard accepts bounded communication latency as a trade-off for field-level operational continuity and record preservation. This trade-off aligns with resilience engineering principles, which distinguish between robustness (resisting disruption) and recoverability (restoring function after disruption); Baranguard's offline-first architecture optimizes for the latter. The system is not designed to eliminate communication gaps during complete network failures, but to ensure that upon reconnection, the incident record queue is reconciled, consistent, and actionable by barangay officials.

        2 Theoretical Framework

        This chapter synthesizes existing studies, systems, and literature that are relevant to the Baranguard system. It establishes the academic foundation of the research, identifies the gaps in current technological implementations, and presents the conceptual framework and definition of terms used throughout the study.

        2.1 Review of Related Literature (RRL)

        The literature reviewed for this study was identified through searches of Google Scholar, the Philippine E-Journals database, and the official issuance repositories of the DILG and DICT, using combinations of the terms barangay information system, incident reporting, emergency dispatch, offline-first, SMS fallback, data privacy, and records custody. The search covered material published between 2020 and 2026 and was restricted to English- and Filipino-language sources. Claims regarding the absence of particular capabilities are therefore scoped to the systems identified within this review and are not presented as exhaustive statements about all deployed systems.

        2.1.1 Digitalization of Barangay Information and Incident Reporting Systems

        The digitalization of local government units has become a paramount objective to enhance public service delivery and governance transparency. In the Philippines, the barangay serves as the fundamental administrative unit, yet many continue to rely on manual, paper-based systems for incident reporting, which often leads to operational inefficiencies and data mismanagement. Longcop and Fuentes (2024) assessed Barangay Health Emergency Response Teams (BHERTs) in Northern Samar, finding that reliance on manual tracking severely limits emergency response capabilities and highlighting the necessity for modernized equipment and data systems. Addressing these administrative bottlenecks, Tosper et al. (2025) developed WebYu, a web-based barangay information management system for Barangay Yuson. Their evaluation using the ISO 25010 standard demonstrated that digitizing resident records and certificate issuance reduced processing times by 68% and documentation errors by 72%.

        Similarly, Gasmido et al. (2025) created a web-based platform using the Agile Scrum framework, achieving an excellent overall ISO 25010 evaluation mean of 4.62, demonstrating that digital centralization improves LGU workflows. Beyond basic administrative tasks, Lardera and Bagolong (2026) introduced a system integrating automated mobile messaging, which allowed barangay officials to send real-time SMS notifications for announcements and emergency alerts, thereby strengthening citizen participation and response coordination. Furthermore, Villafuerte (2024) examined the public safety challenges faced by barangay public safety officers (BPSOs) in the National Capital Region, noting that despite high effectiveness in current measures, critical challenges remain in community engagement, communication infrastructure, and policing strategies.

        The reviewed studies demonstrate that transitioning from manual blotters to web-based information systems improves administrative efficiency and data accuracy in local government units. However, they highlight a critical need to extend digital platforms beyond static record-keeping by integrating network-resilient mobile communication and emergency dispatch tools tailored for frontline peacekeepers and responders operating in low-connectivity environments, which directly supports the development of the Baranguard system.

        2.1.2 Resilient Communications: Offline-First Architecture and SMS Fallback

        Emergency situations and natural disasters frequently compromise traditional terrestrial networks, rendering standard cloud-dependent applications useless during critical response periods. New Light Technologies (2024) emphasized that in disaster zones, an offline-first mobile architecture is essential. By treating disconnected operation as the primary mode rather than a fallback, offline-first designs allow field personnel to continuously capture incident data and access cached geographic maps regardless of internet availability. Complementing this architectural approach, Omar and Van Belle (2023) investigated alternative communication technologies during crises. Their findings revealed the efficacy of utilizing Short Message Service (SMS) as a reliable alternative when IP-based platforms fail due to broadband disruptions, while also noting that SMS itself is subject to failure during the most severe disaster events. Omar et al. (2024) likewise examined frameworks for resilient messaging in emergency scenarios, extending the discussion from SMS toward richer messaging channels. The integration of resilient protocols is further supported by Sasane et al. (2026), who developed an IoT-based disaster management system that paired a cloud-hosted backend with SMS incident reporting and offline-capable mobile dashboards.

        Budimir et al. (2021) highlighted that the widespread ownership of mobile phones in developing nations presents opportunities for disaster risk management, provided that systems are designed to operate under infrastructural constraints. Furthermore, Qureshi et al. (2021) noted that critical incident management systems must securely and efficiently process mobile reports even during high-stress, low-connectivity events.

        The synthesized literature establishes that persistent internet connectivity cannot be assumed during disaster response operations. The studies validate that combining an offline-first mobile architecture with an SMS best-effort fallback protocol is a well-supported strategy for maintaining uninterrupted data capture in partial-outage scenarios, while local SQLite data caching and post-reconnection batch synchronization provide the last line of resilience during full blackout events, directly informing the resilient design of the Baranguard application.

        2.1.3 Data Privacy, Records Custody, and Access Control

        The digitization of incident records introduces data privacy risks, because an incident narrative can contain the names, addresses and circumstances of complainants, respondents and victims. The Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173) mandates that government agencies and private entities secure personally identifiable information (PII) in their information systems (National Privacy Commission, 2012). In a barangay the Barangay Secretary is the keeper of the records of the sangguniang barangay and the barangay assembly (RA 7160, Sec. 394(d)(1)); the law does not name incident narratives, so this study assigns their custody to the Secretary by design, and a system that handles them has to respect who may read, edit and keep them, not only how they are stored.

        Two strands of the literature inform how Baranguard handles this. First, Qureshi et al. (2021) argue that critical incident management systems must be privacy-preserving as well as efficient, which supports designs in which access to sensitive incident content is restricted by role and every access is attributable. Second, Perante-Calina (2026) frames digital governance in local government around transparency, accountability, participation and equity, which is consistent with keeping an audit trail of who acted on a record and with keeping a named officer accountable for each decision. Baranguard applies these ideas by confining the confidential narrative to the Secretary and, on the web only, the Chief Tanod, by never sending it to push notifications, SMS or logs, by recording only identifiers and statuses in the audit log, and by permanently destroying the narrative after a fixed retention period.

        The literature reviewed also shows that records systems in local government are usually studied as repositories, while the question of how an emergency tool should coexist with the official case records is rarely addressed. Baranguard takes a deliberate position: the DILG's BIMS, with its Katarungang Pambarangay module, remains the system for the case process (DILG, 2025), and Baranguard concentrates on the operational layer that BIMS does not provide, namely dispatch, responder tracking, SOS and offline field capture.

        2.2 Gap Bridged by the Study

        Despite numerous deployments of web-based barangay information systems across the Philippines, a significant gap remains in the integration of resilient emergency dispatch protocols for rural local government units. Existing systems are predominantly designed as static administrative repositories for resident profiling and certificate issuance, which fail to operate effectively during natural disasters when internet connectivity is severed. This leaves rural communities highly vulnerable during typhoons and grid failures, where rapid response is critical but communication infrastructure is severely compromised. Furthermore, the field responders who carry out the response, the tanods, are mostly volunteers who receive an honorarium, work without a daily time record and report their work in a monthly accomplishment report, so a useful tool must also fit how their duty is scheduled, reported and signed.

        This study bridges this gap by developing the Baranguard system, which adds an active, offline-first emergency dispatch layer beside the records that barangays already keep. The mobile application caches data locally in an embedded encrypted SQLite database and employs an SMS best-effort fallback mechanism for transmitting an emergency alert with coordinates during partial broadband outages. This design is intended to preserve locally captured incident records during temporary network interruptions and to synchronize them once connectivity is restored. By leveraging cellular network redundancy, the system maintains a vital lifeline between responders and the command center when standard internet pipelines fail. The central services run on a single administrative workstation inside the barangay, so the dashboard remains reachable over the local network during an internet outage. Consequently, the platform is operationally resilient and supports compliance with applicable data privacy requirements under RA 10173 through access controls, an identifier-only audit log and a fixed retention period, while remaining tailored to the geographical and organizational context of the Philippine barangay. Baranguard supports this level indirectly rather than computationally: the system aggregates historical incident records into spatial heatmaps and descriptive trend reports, from which barangay officials form their own judgments about patrol scheduling and resource placement. The system performs no predictive modeling and generates no forecasts; the projection function in Endsley's model is exercised by the human decision-maker, informed by the retrospective evidence the system presents.

        2.4 Concept of the Study

        The structural development and data flow of the Baranguard system operate on a comprehensive Input-Process-Output (IPO) conceptual model. This framework illustrates the transformation of raw operational inputs into structured, actionable information through a locally hosted, resilient processing infrastructure.

        Input: The system receives data from two primary vectors. The first is administrative data, which includes the barangays, user accounts and devices, the school inventory, rosters and the barangay maps. The second is operational data, comprising incident reports logged by staff through the web form (walk-in, phone call, Facebook message or SMS), field observations and incident narratives captured by tanods on the mobile application, GPS coordinates and duty status transmitted or queued by responder devices, and SOS alerts sent over data or by SMS.

        Process: The core processing layer runs on the unified administrative workstation within the barangay command center. The centralized database and the web dashboard are hosted on this single machine, which remains reachable over the barangay local area network during internet outages. Within this environment, the following mechanisms execute:

        A. Offline-first sync and SMS decoding. The mobile application synchronizes cached SQLite data to the command center server when a network path is available, whether over cellular data, broadband, or local Wi-Fi. Synchronization is idempotent: a (device_id, client_event_id) pair prevents duplicate records when a batch is retried after an interrupted upload. During partial outages, a gateway phone attached to the workstation receives the SMS fallback messages, and the backend decodes them into structured entries after verifying and decrypting the envelope.

        B. Dispatch and routing. The Chief Tanod assigns an incident to a tanod. At night, between 18:00 and 06:00, the system instead sends a dispatch offer to the on-duty tanods in rounds, and the first tanod who accepts is assigned. A road route from the tanod's position to the incident is requested from OpenRouteService when needed; the stored route is reused for a short time, and a straight-line distance is shown, labelled as such, when routing is unavailable.

        C. Access control and audit. Every request is checked for role, barangay and ownership on the server. Writes from tanod phones are idempotent and signed by a key held on the device. The audit log records identifiers and statuses only, never narratives, credentials or coordinates.

        D. Rostering and reporting. The Chief Tanod builds and publishes the shift roster, tanods record accomplishment entries, and the Secretary prepares reports. These are produced as A4 printouts that officials sign on paper.

        Output: The culmination of processing yields: dispatch assignments, offers and push alerts delivered to responder devices; a dispatch queue, live map, SOS panel and incident records for the command center; historical incident and hazard heatmaps supporting patrol planning; printed rosters, accomplishment reports and school-zone reports for paper signing; and an audit log of who acted on which record.

        2.5 Definition of Terms

        The following key terms are defined conceptually and operationally as applied within this capstone project:

        Accomplishment report. The monthly report in which a tanod lists the work done, with the confirmed duration of each entry. In this study it is the tanod's record of service, printed and signed on paper, and it is not an automatic attendance record.

        Agile Scrum methodology. An iterative software engineering framework that emphasizes continuous improvement and rapid delivery through short development cycles. In this study, it serves as the specific lifecycle adopted to build, test, and refine the system in collaboration with Pilar barangay officials.

        Barangay. The basic political and administrative unit of government in the Philippines responsible for public service delivery. Identifies the primary deployment sites for the system: Barangays Dao, Binanuahan, Marifosque, and Banuyo in Pilar, Sorsogon.

        Baranguard. Combines "Barangay" and "Vanguard" to represent a frontline defense and administrative response unit in local communities. The official name of the proposed local-server based, offline-first emergency dispatch software developed in this project.

        BIMS (Barangay Information Management System). The information system of the Department of the Interior and Local Government that the DILG provides to barangays under its Local Government Unit Support System (LGUSS), including a Katarungang Pambarangay module. Baranguard complements it and does not replace it.

        Chief Tanod. The head of the barangay tanods. In the system the Chief Tanod uses the Admin account to dispatch tanods, build rosters and manage users and devices, but is not a responder in the system.

        Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173). A Philippine legislative framework designed to protect all forms of private, personal, or sensitive information. Establishes the statutory obligations that the system's privacy controls are designed to support. The confidential narrative of an incident is readable only by the Secretary and, on the web, the Chief Tanod, and is destroyed after one year.

        Dispatch. The act of sending personnel, equipment, or resources to a specific destination to execute an emergency or administrative response. The core mechanism within the command center that allows the Chief Tanod to assign a tanod to an incident and provides GPS routing.

        Dispatch offer. At night, a request sent to the on-duty tanods that is accepted by the first tanod who responds while online, who is then assigned to the incident.

        Incident record. The operational record of a reported matter in Baranguard, with its type, priority, status, location, report channel and, for the Secretary, a confidential narrative. It is not the barangay's official blotter entry.

        Intelligence. In emergency management, curated, actionable information collected and analyzed to support decision-making. Represents the analytical capabilities of the system, which consolidate reports, positions and history into a dispatch queue, live map, heatmaps and statistics for officials.

        ISO/IEC 25010:2023. The international standard for software product quality, which defines characteristics such as functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, interaction capability, reliability, security, maintainability, flexibility, and safety, acts as the formal evaluation instrument used by IT experts and end-users to rate the technical integrity and user acceptance of the deployed system.

        Local-server deployment. A hosting arrangement in which application and database services run on hardware physically located at the point of use rather than on remote infrastructure. In Baranguard, the database and the web dashboard operate on a single administrative workstation inside the barangay command center, which preserves system availability over the local area network during internet outages.

        Offline-first architecture. A software design paradigm in which an application functions primarily without an active internet connection by storing data locally in an embedded database. Defines the structural framework of the mobile application, enabling field Tanods to capture incidents, record GPS coordinates, and access pre-cached MBTiles map packages during network disconnections, with offline records queued in SQLite for automatic synchronization once server connectivity is restored.

        Paper-first signing. The practice, adopted in this study, that rosters, accomplishment reports and school-zone reports are printed and signed on paper outside the application. The application does not record signatures or approvals.

        Personally identifiable information (PII). Any data that could potentially identify a specific individual, either directly or indirectly, such as names or home addresses. Refers to the sensitive data in incident narratives that the system's access controls, audit rules and retention rule are designed to protect.

        Roster. The schedule of shifts of the tanods. It is drafted by the Chief Tanod, published to the tanods, and printed for signing.

        SMS best-effort fallback. A communication mechanism that utilizes standard cellular networks to transmit a critical alert during broadband internet outages. While optimized for partial-outage scenarios as a non-guaranteed protocol, complete network blackouts are instead managed by the offline-first SQLite cache.

        Tanod. A barangay peace-and-order volunteer who responds to incidents in the field. The only role that uses the Android application.

        3.2.0 Baseline Measurement

        Prior to Sprint 0, the research team conducted structured observation sessions and semi-structured interviews with tanods and barangay officials across the four target barangays to establish pre-intervention performance baselines. These baseline metrics serve as the quantitative comparator against which post-deployment ISO 25010:2023 evaluation scores and self-reported efficiency improvements are contextualized.

        The following metrics were collected from each barangay through a minimum of two observation sessions and three staff interviews per barangay:

        1. Incident recording time: The average time (in minutes) required for a tanod or staff member to manually write and file a complete incident entry from the moment an incident is verbally reported, including time spent locating the correct logbook, writing the narrative, and obtaining a supervisor signature.
        2. Tanod dispatch time: The average time (in minutes) for the Chief Tanod to identify an available tanod, contact them via mobile application alert or SMS, and confirm their deployment to an incident location.
        3. Data integrity error rate: The number of documented data loss or legibility incidents per quarter, including lost logbook pages, water-damaged entries, illegible handwriting flagged during audit, and entries with missing required fields.
        4. Post-deployment comparison (applies to the three metrics above): The same three metrics were re-measured after the deployment period. Dispatch coordination time was captured from system timestamps rather than by observation, specifically, the interval between dispatch creation and responder acknowledgement, and incident recording time was captured by timing a staff member entering a standard test incident, which removes observer effects from the post-intervention measure. Because the pre-intervention measure is observational and the post-intervention measure is instrumented, the two are not statistically equivalent; the comparison is reported as an indicative operational change rather than a controlled experimental result.

        3.3.2 Evaluation Instrument and Validity

        The primary research instrument was a structured survey questionnaire anchored on the ISO/IEC 25010:2023 Systems and Software Quality Requirements and Evaluation (SQuaRE) model. This study adopts the nine-characteristic product quality model defined in ISO/IEC 25010:2023. The 2023 revision is used rather than the 2011 edition because it introduces Safety as a top-level quality characteristic. Given that Baranguard dispatches first responders to active emergency scenes, where an incorrect route or an undelivered dispatch alert could place a Tanod in physical danger, Safety is a necessary evaluation dimension for this system and could not be assessed under the 2011 model. To ensure semantic clarity and prevent technical jargon from confounding the data, the questionnaire items were contextualized into local administrative terminology, written in English with a Filipino version, and pre-tested for clarity.

        The questionnaire assessed the system across the nine characteristics of the 2023 model: Functional Suitability, Performance Efficiency, Compatibility, Interaction Capability, Reliability, Security, Maintainability, Flexibility, and Safety. Because "Interaction Capability" and "Flexibility" are unfamiliar terms to non-technical respondents, questionnaire items for these characteristics were phrased in operational language (ease of use and learnability; adaptability across devices and deployment settings) while retaining the standard's definitions for analysis and reporting. Separate forms were prepared for four respondent groups: tanods, the Secretary and Chief Tanod, the Punong Barangay and Kagawad, and IT practitioners and faculty, so that each group answers only the items it has a basis to judge. Prior to full-scale deployment, the instrument underwent content validation by a panel of software engineering experts and disaster risk reduction professionals to verify that each metric accurately mapped to the operational realities of local government units.

        3.3.3 Test Data and Measurement Procedure

        To keep every measurement reproducible and free of real personal data, a specific procedure was applied to the technical tests.

        Test data. All technical tests used synthetic data. Automated test suites each create a disposable database and a throwaway application user, apply the full chain of numbered migrations, run against a temporary server on a spare port, and delete everything afterward, so that the real database is never touched. For measurements and demonstrations, a separate demonstration database was seeded with synthetic incidents, dispatches, GPS tracks and rosters whose coordinates were placed on real roads inside the Barangay Dao boundary. No real resident data or real blotter records from the target barangays were used.

        Response-time measurement. A script logs in once as the Chief Tanod and then times successive read requests, one after another, to the dispatch, incident, GPS, summary and heatmap endpoints. It reports the median, 95th percentile and maximum in milliseconds after a stated warm-up, together with the number of requests and the machine on which it was run. Because it runs on the workstation, it measures server time without the network, and it does not measure rendering or many simultaneous users.

        Delivery and synchronization measurement. Delay figures are computed from timestamps the system already stores: the difference between the time a GPS fix was recorded on the phone and the time the server stored it, the difference between the time an incident was created offline and the time it was synchronized, and the success, duplicate and failure counts of each synchronization batch. These figures are reported only when they come from real use of the system; figures computed from seeded data are shown as seed data and are not cited as results.

        Dispatch response time. Following the definition used throughout the system, the response time of an incident is the interval from the creation of the incident record to the earliest arrival of any responder on it, counted once per incident.

        3.3.4 Acceptance Criteria

        The Baranguard system was considered ready for deployment when all of the following criteria were met:

        1. All user stories in the product backlog successfully pass UAT validation.
        2. The system achieves a weighted mean of at least 4.21 (Excellent) in the overall ISO/IEC 25010:2023 evaluation across all nine quality characteristics.
        3. The SMS best-effort fallback protocol successfully transmits the encoded coordinate payload in at least 19 out of 20 (95%) simulated broadband disconnection scenarios, across three distinct Android device models, under conditions where cellular signal strength is at or above −100 dBm. The exact 95% confidence interval of the observed rate is reported with the result, because 20 trials give a wide interval. The threshold reflects the best-effort nature of the SMS channel rather than the absolute guarantee implied by a 100% criterion.
        4. Records captured offline are synchronized to the server exactly once in at least 95% of the test batches, with every retried or conflicting batch resolved without a duplicate record.
        5. The 95th percentile response time of the key dashboard read endpoints is below two seconds on the administrative workstation.
        6. No endpoint returns a record of another barangay or a confidential narrative to a role that may not read it in the security tests.

        3.4 Ethical Considerations

        This study adhered to the following ethical guidelines throughout its development and testing phases:

        Informed consent. Written consent was obtained from all 27 respondents prior to their participation in user acceptance testing and evaluation activities.

        Data minimization and privacy (RA 10173). No actual historical resident data or real blotter records from the target barangays were used during software development or evaluation. All testing was conducted using entirely synthetic, fabricated data generated by the research team to ensure zero risk of real-world privacy breaches. The system uses no artificial intelligence service; the only data that leaves the workstation for a third party are the origin and destination coordinates of a route request to OpenRouteService, map tiles requested from OpenStreetMap, and push notifications through Firebase Cloud Messaging, none of which carries an incident narrative.

        Voluntary participation. Participation in the evaluation was completely voluntary. Respondents were informed of their right to withdraw from testing scenarios at any time without consequence.

        Data handling during deployment testing. Where user acceptance testing was conducted using the deployed system, all records created during testing were flagged as test data and excluded from the operational records. The audit log records identifiers and action statuses only and does not retain raw personal information, consistent with the record-keeping requirements of the Implementing Rules and Regulations of RA 10173. Confidential narratives are destroyed automatically after one year, and the retention periods for the other record types are provisional until confirmed by the barangay and the Commission on Audit.

        4.3 The Baranguard Barangay Intelligence and Emergency Dispatch System

        This section presents the system as it was built, from the whole to the parts and from design to evidence. The built system departs from the design first proposed in three places, each for a stated reason. The electronic blotter with automated redaction by a local language model was removed, because the barangays keep the blotter in binders and the DILG provides BIMS for the case process, and without a blotter there was nothing for the model to process. The public citizen report form and the Citizen Reports inbox were removed, because residents report through the barangay's existing channels and staff log the report with its channel. Signature approval inside the application was replaced by printing and signing on paper, because that is how the officials already approve. All figures that show screens use the demonstration database and fictitious names.

        4.3.1 System Overview and Architecture

        Baranguard is a local-server-based, offline-first system for barangay incident handling and emergency dispatch. Its users are the Chief Tanod, the Barangay Secretary and the Punong Barangay on a web dashboard, and the tanods on an Android application. It replaces the telephone-and-logbook dispatch described in Figure 4-1 for the operational steps of reporting, assigning, tracking and reporting, while the official blotter stays in the barangay's binders. Figure 4-2 presents the architecture.

        The system has three tiers. In the client tier, the web dashboard is plain JavaScript with a vendored map library and the Android application is built with Ionic React and Capacitor, storing its data in an encrypted SQLite database with an offline queue. In the workstation tier, Apache serves the dashboard and the API, a PHP 8.2 application implements 103 routes in 27 route files, and MariaDB holds 34 tables created by 45 numbered migrations. Evidence files, map packages and encrypted backups are kept outside the web root, scheduled jobs handle backup, retention and the night dispatch offers, and a daemon reads incoming SOS messages from the gateway phone. External services are used only where no local alternative exists: a Cloudflare tunnel for access from outside the workstation, Firebase for push messages, OpenRouteService for road routes and OpenStreetMap for map tiles.

        The components communicate by HTTPS and JSON, except the SMS path, which carries the encrypted SOS from a tanod phone over the cellular network to the gateway phone that is attached to the workstation. The arrangement was chosen so that the core functions need only the workstation: data lives on one machine under the barangay's control, which supports the data privacy requirement, and the field application keeps working without a connection and synchronizes later. The cost of this choice is a single point of failure, which Section 4.4.5 discusses.

        4.3.2 Process and Data Models

        The data flow diagrams follow the Kendall and Kendall notation: a double square is an external entity, a rounded rectangle is a process, an open-ended rectangle is a data store, and an arrow is a data flow named with a noun. Each entity is connected only to the process, no two entities are connected to each other, and every flow has a direction. Residents do not appear because they report through staff and do not use the system directly.

        Figure 4-4 presents Diagram 0, the explosion of the context process into seven processes, named by verb and noun: record incident reports, dispatch assigned tanods, synchronize offline records, relay SOS alerts, track and analyze incidents, prepare roster and reports, and control access and audit. Each process has at least one input and one output, and each data store, D1 to D7, is connected to at least one process; stores are reached only through processes and never directly by an entity.         4.3.4 System Modules and Walkthrough

        The modules are presented in the order a user meets them. For each screen the walkthrough states the input, the processing and the output. All screens show simulated test data (see Section 3.3.3), so counts, response times and positions on them demonstrate that the screens work and are not measured results of real use. The header notice "Some services need attention" is the system reporting honestly that push notifications and the gateway phone were not set up on the machine used for the screenshots (see Service Health).

        Figure 4-8 presents the login screen. The user enters a username and a password. The server checks the credentials, limits repeated failed attempts, and issues a session that lasts 15 minutes of inactivity on the web and is checked against the session table on every request, so a suspended or signed-out account stops working at once. The user then lands on the home screen allowed for the role; a tanod who signs in on the web sees a page stating that the web has no screen for the role.

        Figure 4-9 presents the Admin dashboard of the Chief Tanod. It needs no input. The system counts the pending and reopened incidents, groups the incidents by day and by type, counts the tanods on duty and computes the average response time from the stored arrival times. The Chief Tanod gets a one-screen picture of the barangay's current load.

        Figure 4-10 presents the Dispatch Center. The Chief Tanod, or the Secretary as back-up dispatcher, selects an incident from the emergency queue and chooses an on-duty tanod, or at night sends an offer to the on-duty tanods. Before saving, the system checks that the tanod is not already assigned, that a published shift covers the time unless an override reason is given, and that the 12-hour daily limit is respected. It then stores the dispatch, sets the incident to Dispatched, sends a push alert and SMS to the tanod, and shows the assignment, the incident pin and the route on the map. This screen implements FR-03 and addresses problem P2 of Table 4-1.

        Figure 4-11 presents the Live Map. The screen needs no input and refreshes by polling every 15 seconds. The system returns the latest GPS fix of each on-duty tanod and the age of that fix, and draws them with the open incidents, the SOS alerts and the landmarks inside the barangay boundary. The Chief Tanod sees where each tanod was last reported, and how old that report is, so the position is never presented as more current than it is. This screen addresses problem P3.

        Figure 4-12 presents Incident Management. The staff member searches by text and filters by status, priority and date. The system returns only the records of the user's own barangay, with the incident code, location, priority, status, assigned tanod and the report channel shown as a badge. The list has no narrative, which is shown only on the detail screen to the roles allowed to read it.

        Figure 4-13 presents the Log an Incident form. The staff member selects the type, the priority and the report channel, types the landmark, optionally the complainant's name and the nearby school, and describes the incident. The system validates the fields, stores the incident as Pending under a code of the form INC-YYYY-NNN, and writes the narrative to its own protected column. The incident appears in the list and the Dispatch Center queue at once. The form implements FR-01.

        Figure 4-14 presents the incident detail pane. It shows the location, the time and source of the report, the assigned tanod, the description and the timeline. The Chief Tanod and the Secretary, as back-up dispatcher, may assign a tanod or resolve the incident, and the Secretary, who is also the records custodian, may mark a record as duplicate, invalid, cancelled or reopened. A duplicate is linked to the original and never deleted. The description is shown only to the Secretary and to the Chief Tanod on the web, and each reading is written to the audit log with identifiers only.

        Figure 4-15 presents the Scheduler of the Personnel module. The Chief Tanod or the Secretary adds a standard shift to a tanod on a day of the week grid, or copies the previous week, and the grid shows the availability each tanod gave, the hours per tanod and warnings. The system rejects a shift that would put a tanod over 12 hours in a day, saves new shifts as drafts, and shows them to tanods only after the week is published. Availability is optional: a tanod who gave none can still be scheduled.

        Figure 4-16 presents the Reports tab of Analytics. The official chooses a date range. The system counts the incidents and the resolved share, computes the average response time as the interval from creation to the earliest arrival per incident over the incidents that had an arrival, and states the busiest day, the commonest type and the peak hour. The screen can be printed or exported. It implements the statistical part of FR-06 and stays within the oversight role of the Punong Barangay, who sees counts and trends but no case list.

        Figure 4-17 presents the Heatmap tab. The official chooses the period and the incident category. The system plots every incident that has a location on the map of the barangay, inside its boundary, and ranks the categories. The screen states that it shows historical patterns only and is not a prediction. The result helps decide where to place patrols and implements the heatmap part of FR-06.

        Figure 4-18 presents the Approvals screen. The official sees what waits for a decision: shifts awaiting publication, accomplishment reports that are prepared, and the Annex D term report to prepare. The system states the paper-first sequence, which is to print, sign on paper and then publish the week, and records the result of the publication. This screen addresses problem P5 and reflects the decision that the signed paper is the approval.

        Figure 4-19 presents the Accomplishment Reports screen. The Secretary or an official with the proper authority filters the monthly reports of the tanods by month and status. Each report is built from the entries the tanod encoded on the phone, with the duration the tanod confirmed. The system compares each duration with the time computed from the tanod's duty status and flags the entries that differ by more than 30 minutes, which are shown in the Flagged column. The report is printed for signing, or returned to the tanod with a reason.

        Figure 4-20 presents the Safer School Zones module. The Secretary encodes each school with its classification and focal person, and the system prints the Annex B inventory, the Annex C-1 summary per incident and the Annex D term report from live counts. The printouts carry a Draft layout notice, because the circular's own text was not available to the team when the layouts were made.

        Figure 4-21 presents the Referral Log. It lists the incidents that were referred to the police, the fire service, emergency medical services and other offices, with the date and the agency's reference number. The Lupon or Katarungang Pambarangay is one of the destinations, as a barangay-level office. When the data connection is lost, the SOS button sends an encrypted SMS with the last known position to the gateway phone, and a plain message to one backup contact. The Chief Tanod's phone console also has a Log an incident form for reports that arrive by phone call or Facebook message when the Chief Tanod is away from the desk: it is saved on the phone first and sent when there is a connection. These screens implement FR-01, FR-02 and FR-05 and address problem P4.

        4.3.5 Business Rules and Key Algorithms

        Table 4-10 presents the non-obvious rules the system enforces. Each rule is enforced on the server, so no client can bypass it, and each is exercised by at least one of the automated suites in Section 4.3.8.

        Table 4-10. Business Rules and Key Algorithms
        CodeRule
        BR-01An incident code has the form INC-YYYY-NNN. It is numbered per barangay and per Manila year as the highest existing number plus one, inside the insert's transaction, so a purged incident's number is never issued again.
        BR-02An incident may have several concurrent active dispatches. Cancelling a dispatch needs a reason and returns the incident to Pending only if no other active dispatch remains.
        BR-03A dispatch needs a published shift covering the current time, unless an override reason is given. No tanod may be scheduled for more than 12 hours in a Manila day.

        BR-06An SOS is never refused for a missing GPS fix. It uses the live coordinates, else the last known fix labelled as such, else it is stored with no location.
        BR-07A resource of another barangay, or one the user does not own, answers as not found and never as forbidden, so its existence is not confirmed.
        BR-08The Admin resolves a dispatched incident. A pending or reopened incident that needed no dispatch can be resolved only with a reason of 1 to 255 characters.
        BR-09Only the Secretary marks an incident duplicate, invalid, cancelled or reopened. A duplicate is linked and never deleted, and a closed state can be left only by reopening.
        BR-10A stored route is reused when the same tanod asks again within 90 seconds and 40 metres. After an outage or an exhausted quota the routing service is not called for 120 seconds, and the screen shows the last route as stale or a labelled straight line.
        BR-11An accomplishment entry must be dated no later than today and no more than 62 days back, with a duration of 1 to 1,440 minutes. It is flagged when it differs from the duration computed from duty status by more than 30 minutes.
        BR-12A confidential narrative is permanently destroyed after one year. A legal hold stops all destruction. The retention periods of the newer tables are provisional and their purge is off.
        BR-13A web session ends after 15 minutes of inactivity. A tanod phone session lasts 24 hours of activity with a hard limit of 7 days. Both end on the next request after sign-out, suspension or a password change.
        BR-14Audit entries hold identifiers and statuses only, never a narrative, a credential, a coordinate or a personal detail.
        BR-15The Secretary is the back-up dispatcher: on the web the Secretary may assign or cancel a dispatch, open or cancel a night offer, resolve an incident and acknowledge or resolve an SOS. The Secretary has no phone device session, and all of these actions are audited like the Admin's.
        BR-16A report logged from the Chief Tanod's phone is written to the encrypted phone store first, sent with its own idempotency key, and removed from the phone once the server confirms. A resend never creates a second incident.

BR-03 and BR-04 replace the series of telephone calls with a rule that always yields one assigned tanod, BR-05 and BR-06 make the field application safe to use at the edge of the network, and BR-07, BR-12 and BR-14 are the controls behind the data privacy requirement NFR-03.

        4.4.5 Discussion and Limitations

        Against the objectives. The first three objectives are met in the sense that each was designed and implemented and that its behavior is verified by automated checks (Table 4-13). They are not yet shown to work in the conditions that motivated them. Whether the offline cycle and the SMS fallback work on field phones during a real outage, and whether tanods and officials find the system usable, are questions that only the field measurements, the acceptance test and Table 4-22 can answer. The fourth objective is prepared and not met.

        Against the literature. The reviewed systems were administrative repositories (Tosper et al., 2025; Gasmido et al., 2025), and their evaluations reported ISO 25010 means above 4.5 for record-keeping tasks. Baranguard addresses the gap those studies left: dispatch, tracking and resilient communication for responders. It follows the recommendation of New Light Technologies (2024) to treat disconnected operation as the primary mode, and the finding of Omar and Van Belle (2023) that SMS is a useful but fallible alternative, which is why the fallback is described as best-effort and limited to emergency alerts.

        Limitations and threats to validity.

        • One workstation per barangay. Each barangay's system runs on one machine. If it is off, only the SMS alert to a backup contact still works, and the paper procedure takes over. A UPS and an offsite backup are recommended, but their cost and installation are outside this study.
        • Synthetic data. Every measurement so far uses synthetic records. Real data will have a different size, shape and noise.
        • One cloud dependency. Road routing depends on a third-party service with a daily request quota. The system degrades to a stored route or a labelled straight line, but a live road route is not guaranteed.
        • Device coverage. The Android application has been run on two phone models. Vendor battery policies on other models may stop background location or delay messages.
        • Thin technical sample. Five characteristics are rated by three IT experts, so their means are indicative only.
        • Sample and evaluator bias. The respondents are chosen purposively. The baseline is observed while the post-deployment figure is instrumented, so the comparison is indicative only.
        • Unconfirmed policies. Retention periods other than the one-year narrative rule are provisional until the barangay and the Commission on Audit confirm them, and the Safer School Zones annexes follow the barangay's own forms until DILG MC 2026-037 is checked.
